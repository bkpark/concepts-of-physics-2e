# Chapter 9 proposed questions: internal answer checks

Internal editorial review only; do not publish as a student answer key. Fixed values below are independently evaluated from the stated model, not copied grading outputs.

## 1. Neutral does not mean uncharged particles (Section 9.2)

Positive and negative constituent charges cancel in the net charge; large populations need not imply a large imbalance.

## 2. Charge transfer and conservation (Section 9.2)

A becomes positive and B negative, with equal magnitudes. Their combined net charge remains zero.

## 3. Counting excess electrons (Section 9.2)

−3.2 × 10⁻⁹ C = −3.2 nC; removal gives +3.2 nC.

## 4. Attraction and repulsion (Section 9.2)

(a) and (b); the force acts at a distance and decreases continuously rather than switching off at a finite distance.

## 5. Why a charged object attracts a polar molecule (Section 9.3)

The molecule turns so its opposite-sign region is nearer. The closer attractive interaction is stronger than the more distant repulsive interaction; net attraction results.

## 6. One Coulomb-force calculation (Section 9.3)

1.26 × 10⁻³ N on each; repulsive, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

## 7. Inverse-square and charge-product predictions (Section 9.3)

(a) 5/9 N ≈ 0.56 N; (b) 10 N; (c) 5.0 N.

## 8. Where can forces cancel? (Section 9.3)

x < 0. Between charges the fields reinforce. Outside, fields oppose, but cancellation must be nearer the smaller-magnitude source. Reversing test-charge sign reverses both forces and leaves the cancellation point unchanged.

## 9. A small test charge (Section 9.4)

Otherwise its own force rearranges the sources, so the measured field is not the original field.

## 10. Field versus force (Section 9.4)

Forces qE east, 2qE east, qE west; the prescribed source field is unchanged.

## 11. Inferring a field from a negative test charge (Section 9.4)

300 N/C downward.

## 12. Force in a known field (Section 9.4)

1.0 × 10⁻³ N east and west, respectively.

## 13. Drawing isolated-charge fields (Section 9.5)

Radially outward from +q, inward toward −3q; three times as many lines for −3q under the same plotting convention.

## 14. Reading field-line diagrams (Section 9.5)

(a), (b) correct; (c) would assign two field directions to one point; (d) confuses force/acceleration direction with velocity and trajectory.

## 15. An unequal pair of opposite charges (Section 9.5)

Lines leave +q and end on −3q; additional lines arrive from infinity at −3q. Far away the net charge is −2q.

## 16. Potential difference means energy per charge (Section 9.6)

12 J/C; ΔPE = qΔV = +24 μJ.

## 17. Choosing the potential reference (Section 9.6)

Potential zero is arbitrary; differences, field, and forces do not change.

## 18. Equal potentials do not require zero field (Section 9.6)

W_electric = −qΔV = 0. No; nonzero forces may do cancelling work, or be perpendicular to displacement.

## 19. Volts and electron volts (Section 9.6)

V is J/C; eV is energy, 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. It is a convenient microscopic scale and can describe any system’s energy.

## 20. Electron acceleration and the energy sign (Section 9.6)

ΔPE = −100 eV = −1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ J; ΔKE = +100 eV = +1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.

## 21. Diagnosing electrostatic equilibrium (Section 9.7)

No: tangential forces drive charge motion.

## 22. Recognizing a uniform electric field (Section 9.7)

The lines are evenly spaced, straight, parallel, and point in the same direction in the central region. Near the plate edges, the lines curve and their spacing changes, so the field is no longer approximately uniform.

## 23. The Van de Graaff belt (Section 9.7)

In the usual design the insulating belt transports deposited charge instead of allowing it to leak along a continuous conducting path. A conducting replacement would not retain charge in the same way.

## 24. Electrostatic shielding (Section 9.7)

Mobile charges redistribute so the field within the conducting material vanishes; the closed empty cavity is equipotential and has zero electrostatic field.

## 25. Hair in an electrostatic demonstration (Section 9.7)

Like-charged strands repel; reversing all signs leaves the repulsion.

## 26. What an ampere measures (Section 9.8)

(a); current is charge-flow rate, not carrier speed or charge consumption.

## 27. Neutral wire, nonzero current (Section 9.8)

Yes. Mobile electrons drift while their negative charge is approximately balanced by positive lattice charge; neutrality does not require no motion.

## 28. Electrons and conventional current (Section 9.8)

East; conventional current follows positive-charge motion, so negative carriers moving west give eastward current.

## 29. A complete circuit (Section 9.8)

A complete conducting path back to the other terminal is needed; charge buildup quickly prevents sustained flow in the open path.

## 30. Charge passing a flashlight (Section 9.8)

600/1800 = 0.333 A.

## 31. Short pulse, small transferred charge (Section 9.8)

0.250 A; charge, potential difference, and the time-dependent discharge conditions determine energy. A large brief current does not specify energy by itself.

## 32. Energy is transferred; charge is conserved (Section 9.9)

Equal currents; charge is conserved and energy is transferred to the resistor, decreasing electric potential energy along conventional current.

## 33. A useful fluid analogy (Section 9.9)

Volume-flow rate corresponds to current; pressure difference to voltage difference. Steady flow carries the same volume per second through the pipe while mechanical energy is transferred.

## 34. Recognizing ohmic behavior (Section 9.9)

Yes; V/I = 20 Ω for each pair; 0.40 A.

## 35. One direct Ohm’s-law calculation (Section 9.9)

0.833 A; filament resistance depends on temperature, so its operating resistance differs from its cold resistance.

## 36. Voltage lost in a cord (Section 9.9)

0.30 V and 1.5 V; the higher-resistance cord leaves less appliance voltage.

## 37. Two power formulas, different constraints (Section 9.10)

At fixed V, P falls as R rises; at fixed I, P rises. V and I cannot both stay fixed while R changes for an ohmic resistor.

## 38. Power from voltage and current (Section 9.10)

6.00 kW; 12 kJ.

## 39. Heating in the same cords (Section 9.10)

1.5 W and 7.5 W; factor of four.

## 40. Kilowatt-hours are energy (Section 9.10)

1000 J/s × 3600 s = 3.60 × 10⁶ J; energy.

## 41. Energy and cost of a short use (Section 9.10)

72,000 J = 0.020 kW·h; $0.0060, or 0.60 cent.

## 42. Battery capacity is not energy by itself (Section 9.10)

2.4 A·h = 8640 C; voltage is also needed; approximately 12,960 J ≈ 13 kJ.

## 43. An ideal switch and its voltage (Section 9.11)

Open: I = 0, full battery voltage across switch, P = 0. Closed: I = V/R, zero switch voltage, P = 0.

## 44. A broken bulb in a series string (Section 9.11)

3 V; all go out; 4 V per remaining bulb, with greater power per bulb under the fixed-resistance model.

## 45. Obtaining a smaller resistance (Section 9.11)

Connect resistors in parallel; equivalent resistance is smaller than each positive branch resistance, though discrete available values do not guarantee an exact target.

## 46. Compare the same resistors in series and parallel (Section 9.11)

Series: 9.0 Ω, 4/3 A in each. Parallel: 2.0 Ω, 2.0 A in 6 Ω and 4.0 A in 3 Ω; 6.0 A total. Parallel draws more.

## 47. Adding a parallel branch (Section 9.11)

Equivalent resistance halves; total source current doubles; original branch current is unchanged.

## 48. Combined appliance load (Section 9.11)

10 A and 5 A; 15 A total. Lamp adds 1 A, bringing total to 16 A, above the nominal rating. Actual protection behavior is not an instantaneous ideal threshold.

## 49. Reject impossible equivalent resistances (Section 9.11)

Both impossible: parallel equivalent must be below 145 Ω, series total above 900 kΩ.

## 50. Two kinds of electrical hazard (Section 9.12)

Thermal effects such as overheating/fire, and shock from current through the body; both may occur in one event.

## 51. A short circuit is not a safety guarantee (Section 9.12)

At a maintained voltage, lower resistance means larger current and heating power; a short does not rule out exposed energized parts or a separate path through a person.

## 52. Why voltage alone is insufficient (Section 9.12)

Current depends on total path resistance and source behavior; effects also depend on current path, duration, waveform/frequency and individual conditions.

## 53. Skin resistance and contact conditions (Section 9.12)

Skin/contact resistance can dominate; wet skin and greater contact area can reduce it. It varies with circumstances, so a fixed assumed value cannot guarantee protection.

## 54. Potential difference across a body (Section 9.12)

Nearby points on one line have small voltage difference; contact across differing potentials can drive substantial current through the body. This does not imply power lines are safe to approach.

## 55. What a circuit breaker protects (Section 9.12)

It interrupts excessive circuit current; dangerous body current can be far below its rating. Bypassing it allows fault/overload heating to continue rather than being interrupted.
