# Chapter 8 question-set proposal

Status: approved and applied on 2026-09-30; see applied-question-manifest.json for final numbering. Current numbers refer to preview version 58. Prepared 2026-09-30.

Recommend **47 questions**: 35 retained or revised existing questions plus 12 MyOpenMath-based additions; 70 existing questions removed. This count follows coverage, not a preset quota. Preserve all five inline Check Your Understanding items.

## Basis and scope

Reviewed the current 105-question inventory, maintained abridged sections 8.2–8.14, and the local September 27 MyOpenMath export. The crosswalk has 81 Chapter 8 records, including test-bank variants and lecture comprehension sets; these are not 81 distinct additions. The proposal favors prediction, explanation, short energy balances, and graph reading. No combinatorial formulas or logarithm calculations are required.

The approved temperature-summary edit remains pending for the next refresh: one Celsius-to-kelvin relation inline, with the section body unchanged. This proposal does not restore Fahrenheit-conversion formulas.

MyOpenMath sources below abbreviate the common “Introduction to Physics” prefix. Ordinary sources occur in **Question Set 7: Thermodynamics** and **Question Set 7 Assessment**, with archived reading/exercise copies. Test-bank variants occur in **Multiple-Choice Timed Assessment for Chapter 8**. Essay sources occur in **Essay Timed Assessment for Chapter 8** and **Take-Home Option A Final Exam**. ## Proposed set, in topic order


### Section 8.2

**1. Thermal equilibrium** (current Exercise 1).

Retain: What does it mean to say that two systems are in thermal equilibrium?

**2. A physical property used by a thermometer** (current Exercise 2).

Retain: Give an example of a physical property that varies with temperature and describe how it is used to measure temperature.

**3. Equilibrium with the room** (current Exercise 4).

A cup of hot water is left in a room whose temperature remains constant. After enough time for thermal equilibrium, how will the temperatures of the water, cup, and room compare? Explain.

**4. One Celsius-to-kelvin calculation** (current Exercise 5).

A person has a temperature of 39.0 °C. What is this temperature in kelvins? Would changing the temperature scale change whether the person has a fever? Explain.

**5. Temperature changes versus temperature readings** (new adaptation).

A sample warms from 20 °C to 30 °C. What is its temperature change in degrees Celsius and in kelvins? Why do its temperature readings differ between the two scales even though the temperature changes have the same numerical value?

Source comparison: Temperature Scales – Definition – Q1.


### Section 8.3

**6. Limits of the ideal-gas model** (current Exercise 13).

Retain: Under what circumstances would you expect a gas to behave significantly differently than predicted by the ideal gas law?

**7. Constant-volume gas thermometer** (current Exercise 14).

Retain: A constant-volume gas thermometer contains a fixed amount of gas. What property of the gas is measured to indicate its temperature?

**8. Ideal-gas predictions** (new adaptation).

For a fixed amount of ideal gas, predict what happens to its temperature when (a) its pressure increases at constant volume, (b) its volume decreases at constant pressure, and (c) its pressure doubles while its volume is halved. Explain each prediction.

Source comparison: Ideal Gas Law – Descriptions – Q2.

**9. Recognizing insufficient information** (new adaptation).

A fixed amount of ideal gas expands. Is that information alone enough to determine whether its temperature increases, decreases, or stays the same? Explain what additional information would allow you to decide.

Source comparison: Ideal Gas Law – Descriptions – Q2; also its test-bank version.

**10. A short pressure–volume calculation** (current Exercise 20).

A sealed flexible bag contains 100 mL of air at an absolute pressure of 100 kPa. The surrounding pressure falls to 75 kPa. Assume the air stays at the same temperature, no air escapes, and the bag expands until its internal pressure equals the surrounding pressure. What is its final volume? Explain why the direction of the change makes sense.


### Section 8.4

**11. Direction of heat transfer** (current Exercise 26).

Retain: How is heat transfer related to temperature?

**12. Is heat stored in an object?** (current Exercise 28).

Retain: When heat transfers into a system, is the energy stored as heat? Explain briefly.

**13. Heat transfer and work can produce similar effects** (current Exercise 27).

Water can become warmer either by contact with a hotter object or by stirring it in an insulated container. How does energy enter the water in each case? What can happen to the water’s internal energy?

Source comparison: Heat – Descriptions – Q1.


### Section 8.5

**14. Recognizing the three transfer mechanisms** (new adaptation).

Identify the main heat-transfer mechanism in each case: (a) energy passes along a stationary metal spoon from its hot end, (b) warm water circulates through a cooler region of a pot, and (c) sunlight warms your skin. Which mechanism can transfer energy across a vacuum?

Source comparison: Heat – Transfer Methods – Q1 and Q2.

**15. Thermos bottle** (current Exercise 31).

A thermos bottle has two walls separated by a vacuum, silvered surfaces that reflect infrared radiation, and a tightly fitting stopper made from a material that conducts heat poorly.

(a) How does the vacuum reduce heat transfer by conduction and convection?

(b) Why can radiation still transfer energy across the vacuum, and how do the silvered surfaces reduce this transfer?

(c) How does the stopper help reduce heat transfer?

(d) Explain why the same thermos can keep a hot drink hot and a cold drink cold.


### Section 8.6

**16. Factors determining heat needed for a temperature change** (current Exercise 32).

Retain: What three factors affect the heat transfer that is necessary to change an object’s temperature?

**17. Specific-heat comparison with a short calculation** (new adaptation).

Equal 1.0-kg samples of water and steel each absorb 4.2 kJ, with no phase change or work done. Use specific heats of 4200 J/(kg·°C) for water and 450 J/(kg·°C) for steel. (a) Predict which sample has the larger temperature rise. (b) Calculate each rise. Explain how your results reflect specific heat.

Source comparison: Heat Capacity – Calculations – Q1; qualitative test-bank version.

**18. Mixing warm and cool water** (new adaptation).

Equal masses of water at 20 °C and 60 °C are mixed in an insulated container of negligible heat capacity. (a) What is the final temperature? (b) If the mass of the cooler water were greater, would the final temperature be above or below your answer to (a)? Explain. Assume no phase change and constant specific heat.

Source comparison: Heat Capacity – Calculations – Q2.

**19. Brake temperature and initial speed** (current Exercise 33).

Retain: The brakes in a car increase in temperature by ΔT when bringing the car to rest from a speed v. How much greater would ΔT be if the car initially had twice the speed? You may assume the car to stop sufficiently fast so that no heat transfers out of the brakes.

**20. Oakland–Orinda temperature comparison** (current Exercise 40).

Oakland, California, lies beside San Francisco Bay and is exposed to cool marine air. Orinda is a short distance inland, east of Oakland, with hills between it and the bay that reduce this marine influence. On a hot summer day, Orinda can be considerably warmer than Oakland. How does the large specific heat of water help explain why the nearby water moderates Oakland’s temperature? Explain why water warms more slowly than land during the day and cools more slowly as temperatures fall.

Source comparison: Heat Capacity – Applications – Q1 (Oakland/Orinda); geographic context added at maintainer request; replaces current Exercise 40.


### Section 8.7

**21. Energy absorbed or released during phase changes** (new adaptation).

For each process, state whether the substance absorbs or releases energy as heat: (a) melting, (b) freezing, (c) vaporization, and (d) condensation. Explain briefly in terms of changes in molecular interactions.

Source comparison: Latent Heat – Descriptions – Q1.

**22. Why temperature can remain constant during heating** (new adaptation).

Heat is transferred into a mixture of ice and liquid water at its melting temperature. Why can the ice melt without the temperature rising? Explain where the added energy goes.

Source comparison: Latent Heat – Descriptions – Q2.

**23. Ice and water in thermal equilibrium** (current Exercise 37).

Ice and liquid water are in thermal equilibrium at 0°C in an insulated container, also at 0°C, at ordinary atmospheric pressure. Will the total amount of ice increase, decrease, or remain the same? Explain. Does this mean that no molecules move between the ice and liquid water?

**24. Condensation on a glass of ice water** (current Exercise 38).

Retain: What effect does condensation on a glass of ice water have on the rate at which the ice melts? Will the condensation speed up the melting process or slow it down?

**25. Read the existing heating curve** (new adaptation).

Use the heating curve in Section 8.7 for the same fixed mass of water at atmospheric pressure. Rank the energy inputs required to (a) warm ice from −20 °C to 0 °C, (b) melt all the ice at 0 °C, (c) warm the liquid from 0 °C to 100 °C, and (d) vaporize all the liquid at 100 °C. Explain how the horizontal lengths of the graph support your ranking.

Source comparison: Latent Heat – Descriptions – Q3; also its test-bank version.

**26. Evaporation and cooling the body** (current Exercise 30).

Sweat on exposed skin can evaporate, while sweat on skin submerged in warm water cannot evaporate directly into the air. Explain why evaporation can cool the body and why this cooling mechanism is unavailable to submerged skin.

Source comparison: Thermal Physics – Cooling Processes (essay question); retain the useful idea from the hot-tub question.


### Section 8.8

**27. First law as conservation of energy** (current Exercise 46).

Retain: The first law of thermodynamics and the conservation of energy are clearly related. How do they differ in the types of energy considered?

**28. Stored internal energy versus transfers by heat and work** (current Exercise 47).

Retain: Heat transfer Q and work done W are always energy in transit, whereas internal energy U is energy stored in a system. Give an example of each type of energy, and state specifically how it is either in transit or resides in a system.

**29. One first-law energy balance** (new adaptation).

A closed system absorbs 500 J as heat and does 200 J of work on its surroundings. (a) How does its internal energy change? (b) Starting from the same initial state, suppose instead that 200 J of work is done on it while it absorbs the same heat. How does its internal energy change now? Explain the difference.

Source comparison: Thermodynamics – First Law – Q1; Lecture 17, Sections 3 and 5.


### Section 8.9

**30. Constant-volume heating** (current Exercise 54).

A fixed amount of gas is heated in a rigid sealed container. Assume there is no work other than possible expansion or compression work. Does the gas do work? What happens to its internal energy? Explain using the first law.

Source comparison: Thermodynamics – Processes – Q2.

**31. Isothermal expansion** (current Exercise 55).

A fixed amount of ideal gas expands isothermally and does work on a piston. Does its internal energy change? Must energy enter or leave as heat? Explain using the first law.

Source comparison: Thermodynamics – Processes – Q3.

**32. Adiabatic expansion** (current Exercise 56).

A fixed amount of ideal gas expands against a piston and does work while no heat is transferred. What happens to its internal energy and temperature? Explain using the first law.

Source comparison: Thermodynamics – Processes – Q4.

**33. When the adiabatic approximation is useful** (current Exercise 57).

Retain: A real process may be nearly adiabatic if it occurs over a very short time. How does the short time span help the process to be adiabatic?


### Section 8.10

**34. Why a heat engine needs a temperature difference** (current Exercise 60).

Retain: Is a temperature difference necessary to operate a heat engine? State why or why not.

**35. Heat-engine work and efficiency** (new adaptation).

During each complete cycle, a heat engine absorbs 600 J from a hot reservoir and releases 400 J to a cold reservoir. (a) How much net work does it do? (b) What is its efficiency? (c) Does the energy released to the cold reservoir disappear? Explain.

Source comparison: Thermodynamics – Efficiency – Q1.


### Section 8.11

**36. Why the drinking bird eventually stops** (current Exercise 63).

Retain: Think about the drinking bird at the beginning of this section (). If left to its own devices over time, the bird will cease “drinking”. Why does its dipping motion eventually stop?

**37. Can engineering eliminate waste heat?** (current Exercise 64).

Retain: Can improved engineering and materials be employed in heat engines to reduce heat transfer into the environment? Can they eliminate heat transfer into the environment entirely?

**38. Second law versus energy conservation** (current Exercise 65).

Retain: Does the second law of thermodynamics alter the conservation of energy principle?

**39. Evaluate an engine claim** (current Exercise 72).

A proposed cyclic heat engine operates between reservoirs at 600 K and 300 K and absorbs 100 J from the hot reservoir each cycle. (a) What is its maximum possible efficiency? (b) Could it deliver 60 J of work per cycle? (c) Would a claim of 40 J per cycle prove that the engine actually works? Explain what the theoretical limit does and does not tell you.


### Section 8.12

**40. Temperature difference and heat-pump performance** (current Exercise 77).

For an ideal heat pump maintaining the same indoor temperature, why does a colder outdoor temperature require more work to deliver the same amount of energy indoors? Relate your answer to the coefficient of performance.

**41. Why an open refrigerator cannot cool the kitchen** (current Exercise 79).

Retain: Can you cool a kitchen by leaving the refrigerator door open?

**42. COP greater than one and conservation of energy** (new adaptation).

A heat pump uses 1.0 kJ of electrical work to deliver 3.0 kJ to a room. (a) How much energy does it take from outdoors? (b) What is its coefficient of performance in heating mode? (c) Explain why delivering more energy than the electrical input does not violate conservation of energy.

Source comparison: Thermal Physics – Heat Pump Coefficient of Performance (essay); Heat Pump – Efficiency – Q1.


### Section 8.13

**43. Freezing and the entropy of the surroundings** (current Exercise 91).

Retain: Explain how water’s entropy can decrease when it freezes without violating the second law of thermodynamics. Specifically, explain what happens to the entropy of its surroundings.

**44. Temperature differences and useful work** (current Exercise 92).

Two bodies at different temperatures exchange heat inside an isolated enclosure until they reach the same temperature. What happens to their combined entropy? Why does reaching a common temperature reduce the opportunity to obtain work from them using a heat engine?

**45. Free expansion and irreversibility** (current Exercise 94).

A gas initially occupies one half of an insulated container; the other half is a vacuum. A partition is removed, and the gas expands freely into the empty half.

(a) Why is this process irreversible?

(b) No heat is transferred during the expansion. Does this mean that the gas’s entropy remains unchanged? Explain why substituting Q = 0 into ΔS = Q_rev/T, the relation for reversible heat transfer at constant temperature, would not correctly determine the entropy change in this free expansion.


### Section 8.14

**46. A small, concrete microstate-counting example** (current Exercise 104).

Three distinguishable fair coins are tossed independently. (a) List the eight possible sequences of heads and tails. (b) How many sequences give exactly two heads? How many give three heads? (c) Which macrostate is more likely, and why? Explain how counting possible microscopic arrangements helps motivate the statistical interpretation of entropy.

**47. Gas volume and available microscopic arrangements** (current Exercise 97).

Retain: Why does a gas have greater entropy when it occupies a larger volume at the same temperature? Explain in terms of the microscopic arrangements available to its molecules.

## Coverage findings and remaining optional choice

- **Thermos (current 31): retain the approved simplified version.** Supply the infrared-reflection property in the question and focus on the vacuum, silvered surfaces, stopper, and keeping drinks hot or cold. Retain its existing cutaway figure. No emissivity definition or formula is required.
- **Statistical interpretation: recommend retain the three-coin exercise.** It follows the section’s explicit microstate model, gives students a manageable counting task, and replaces the large-number/logarithm exercises. If even that feels excessive, ask students to compare counts supplied in the existing five-coin table instead; the count remains 47.
- **Heat transfer:** the new recognition question stays within the definitions actually taught. The existing inline everyday-example question remains; the new question adds explicit classification and vacuum transfer.
- **Climate:** use one water-specific-heat question in 8.6, combining the purpose of current 40 and the Oakland/Orinda MyOpenMath question. Do not add a second regional climate question or imply that water heat capacity alone explains every local weather difference.
- **Phase change:** use the existing heating-curve figure in m67124, ID `import-auto-id2928714`. Confirm its rendered number when implemented. The question specifies the same sample and atmospheric pressure; no new figure is needed.
- **Processes:** the abridged body explicitly teaches isobaric, isochoric, isothermal, and adiabatic processes. The ideal-gas prediction question covers constant pressure, and the three energy-transfer questions cover the other processes. Specify ideal gas and expansion work where needed.
- **Entropy:** the body explicitly says no logarithm calculations are needed. Retain explanation and elementary counting; avoid equating visual messiness with entropy. The free-expansion question links 8.13 to the existing 8.14 gas discussion and distinguishes actual heat transfer from heat transfer along a reversible path; it does not label free expansion adiabatic.
