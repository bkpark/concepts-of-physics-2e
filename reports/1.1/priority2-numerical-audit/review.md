Effects of Acute Whole-Body Radiation Exposure

Approximate absorbed doses for penetrating X-rays or gamma rays delivered to the whole body or most of it within a short time.

Absorbed dose (Gy) | Major effects
About 0.7–10 | Bone-marrow damage reduces blood cell production, increasing the risk of infection and bleeding.
Above about 10 | Severe intestinal damage, in addition to bone-marrow damage; survival is unlikely.
Above about 50 | Severe damage to the circulatory and central nervous systems; rapidly fatal.

These ranges describe the usual full syndromes; some symptoms occur at lower doses, and effects overlap. Multiply Gy by 100 to obtain rad.

**Proposed:** The large-scale effects of radiation on humans include acute radiation sickness and long-term effects. [Table reference] gives the effects of large whole-body doses received in a short time. These effects may develop over days or weeks. Exposure of a small part of the body can cause local tissue injury without whole-body radiation sickness.

## P2-01 — Acute-effects table

Review condensed table and nearby explanation

- A short table can retain the physical dose scale without presenting a clinical prognosis chart.
- The dose ranges describe usual full syndromes, not sharp thresholds or mutually exclusive effects.
- Gy/rad describes absorbed dose for tissue injury; these values are scoped to penetrating X-rays or gamma rays, not all radiation types.

**Recommendation:** Keep the absorbed-dose scale, major tissue effects, and distinctions between short whole-body exposure, fractionated exposure, and localized injury. Remove the detailed fertility row and exact mortality percentage/deadline from this summary.

**Review:** Resolved and applied after maintainer section read-through. No content decision remains.

**Source limits:** CDC explicitly supports these approximate ranges. Some symptoms occur below them; no lower boundary here is a safety threshold. The higher-dose effects do not replace bone-marrow injury. Fertility and survival timing require more context than this summary warrants.

- [CDC: Acute Radiation Syndrome for clinicians](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html) — Conditions for ARS; three classic syndromes; cutaneous radiation injury; dose footnote

**Before:** Any dose less than 100 mSv (10 rem) is called a low dose, 0.1 Sv to 1 Sv (10 to 100 rem) is called a moderate dose, and anything greater than 1 Sv (100 rem) is called a high dose. There is no known way to determine after the fact if a person has been exposed to less than 10 mSv.

**Proposed:** For X-rays and gamma rays, an absorbed dose less than 0.1 Gy (10 rad) is called a low dose, 0.1 to 1 Gy (10 to 100 rad) a moderate dose, and greater than 1 Gy (100 rad) a high dose.

**Before:** a dose less than 100 mSv (10 rem)

**Proposed:** an absorbed dose less than 0.1 Gy (10 rad) for X-rays and gamma rays

**Before:** a dose from 0.1 Sv to 1 Sv (10 to 100 rem)

**Proposed:** an absorbed dose from 0.1 to 1 Gy (10 to 100 rad) for X-rays and gamma rays

**Before:** a dose greater than 1 Sv (100 rem)

**Proposed:** an absorbed dose greater than 1 Gy (100 rad) for X-rays and gamma rays

**Before:** Whole-body, single-exposure doses of 0.1 Sv or less are low doses while those of 0.1 to 1 Sv are moderate, and those over 1 Sv are high doses.

**Proposed:** For X-rays and gamma rays, absorbed doses below 0.1 Gy are called low, those from 0.1 to 1 Gy moderate, and those above 1 Gy high.

### Approved table — applied

Natural Background Radiation: Global Average Annual Effective Dose

Rounded estimates from UNSCEAR’s 2024 assessment. Medical and occupational exposures are not included.

Source | Average annual effective dose (mSv)
Inhalation, mainly radon, thoron, and their decay products | 1.8
Cosmic radiation | 0.3
External radiation from the ground and building materials | 0.4
Ingestion in food and water | 0.5
Total | 3.0

These are global averages; exposure varies with location and living conditions.

**Before:** Even the linear hypothesis estimates of the risks are relatively small, and the average person is not exposed to large amounts of radiation. [Table reference] lists average annual background radiation doses from natural and artificial sources for Australia, the United States, Germany, and world-wide averages. Cosmic rays are partially shielded by the atmosphere, and the dose depends upon altitude and latitude, but the average is about 0.40 mSv/yr. A good example of the variation of cosmic radiation dose with altitude comes from the airline industry. Monitored personnel show an average of 2 mSv/yr. A 12-hour flight might give you an exposure of 0.02 to 0.03 mSv.

**Proposed:** Even the linear hypothesis estimates of the risks are relatively small, and the average person is not exposed to large amounts of radiation. [Table reference] lists global average annual effective doses from natural background radiation, totaling about 3 mSv (300 mrem). Cosmic rays are partially shielded by the atmosphere, and the dose depends upon altitude and latitude. Radon exposure also varies with local geology and the construction and ventilation of buildings.

**Before:** Note the large dose from a CT scan.

**Proposed:** For comparison, a chest X-ray dose of about 0.1 mSv is comparable to about 12 days of natural background at 3 mSv per year; an abdomen and pelvis CT dose of about 7.7 mSv is comparable to about 2.6 years. These comparisons indicate the scale of effective dose, not the same exposure of each organ.

**Before:** These limits do not mark a boundary between harmless and harmful exposure; radiation protection also aims to keep doses as low as reasonably achievable. Extensive monitoring with a variety of radiation detectors is performed to support radiation safety.

**Proposed:** Radiation protection follows the ALARA principle: keep doses as low as reasonably achievable, even when they are below regulatory limits. Radiation detectors are used to monitor exposure and evaluate protective measures.

**Before:** Some places have great natural concentrations of uranium and thorium, yielding doses ten times as high as the average value.

**Proposed:** Some places have great natural concentrations of uranium and thorium, yielding much higher terrestrial radiation doses—for example, areas with thorium-rich sands along the Kerala coast in India.

**Before:** Internal doses come from foods and liquids that we ingest. Fertilizers containing phosphates have potassium and uranium. So we are all a little radioactive. Carbon-14 has about 66 Bq/kg radioactivity whereas fertilizers may have more than 3000 Bq/kg radioactivity.

**Proposed:** Internal doses come from naturally occurring radioactive isotopes in foods and liquids that we ingest, including potassium-40 and carbon-14. So we are all a little radioactive.
