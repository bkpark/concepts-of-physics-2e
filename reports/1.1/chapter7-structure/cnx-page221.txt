The general form of Bernoulli’s equation has three terms in it, and it is broadly applicable. To understand it better, we will look at
a number of specific situations that simplify and illustrate its use and meaning.
Bernoulli’s Equation for Static Fluids
Let us first consider the very simple situation where the fluid is static—that is, v1 = v2 = 0 . Bernoulli’s equation in that case is
(8.62)P1 + ρgh1 = P2 + ρgh2.
We can further simplify the equation by taking h2 = 0 (we can always choose some height to be zero, just as we often have
done for other situations involving the gravitational force, and take all other heights to be relative to this). In that case, we get
(8.63)P2 = P1 + ρgh1.
This equation tells us that, in static fluids, pressure increases with depth. As we go from point 1 to point 2 in the fluid, the depth
increases by h1 , and consequently, P2 is greater than P1 by an amount ρgh1 . In the very simplest case, P1 is zero at the
top of the fluid, and we get the familiar relationship P = ρgh . (Recall that P = ρgh and ΔPEg = mgh. ) Bernoulli’s equation
includes the fact that the pressure due to the weight of a fluid is ρgh . Although we introduce Bernoulli’s equation for fluid flow, it
includes much of what we studied for static fluids in the preceding chapter.
