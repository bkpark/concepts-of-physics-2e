## Exercise 4 — Section 10.3

An unmagnetized iron nail contains magnetic domains. Why does it not necessarily act as a strong magnet? Explain what changes when the nail becomes magnetized and what can happen if it is heated above its Curie temperature.

Internal answer: Differently oriented domains largely cancel. Magnetization favors aligned domains. Above the Curie temperature, thermal disorder destroys ferromagnetic order; previous permanent magnetization is lost. Cooling does not necessarily restore the previous magnetization.

## Exercise 30 — Section 10.10

A coil rotates in a steady magnetic field and supplies a current to a connected resistor. Explain why a voltage can be induced even though the magnetic field is steady. Where does the energy dissipated in the resistor come from?

Internal answer: Rotation changes the flux through the coil. Mechanical work turning the coil supplies the electrical energy dissipated in the resistor; the steady magnetic field is not a free energy supply.

## Exercise 31 — Section 10.10

A closed conducting loop is held fixed while the magnetic field through it increases in strength without changing direction. What is the direction of the magnetic field produced by the induced current relative to the applied field? How does your answer change when the applied field decreases instead?

Internal answer: For increasing applied flux, the induced field opposes the applied field; for decreasing flux, it points in the same direction. In both cases it opposes the change, not necessarily the applied field itself.

## Exercise 32 — Section 10.10

A strong magnet falls through a vertical copper tube much more slowly than it falls through an otherwise similar plastic tube. Copper is not ferromagnetic, but it conducts electricity. Currents induced in the body of a conductor are called eddy currents. (a) Explain why the falling magnet induces currents in the copper tube. (b) Use Lenz’s law to explain why these currents slow the magnet’s fall. (c) As the magnet falls, some of its gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic energy. Where does the rest go?

Internal answer: (a) The moving magnet changes magnetic flux through conducting paths in the tube, inducing voltage and circulating currents by Faraday’s law. (b) Their magnetic effects oppose the flux changes and exert an upward force on the falling magnet. (c) Energy is converted mainly to thermal energy through the electrical resistance of the copper; at terminal speed, kinetic energy is constant and the lost gravitational potential energy goes into heating (neglecting other losses).
